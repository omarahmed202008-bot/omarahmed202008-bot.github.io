Adaptive World Models

Uncertainty signals and adaptive planning under abrupt dynamics changes: a reproducible CPU-scale study of neural transition ensembles, change detection, and finite-horizon control.

Documentation status: Research manuscript revised 21 October 2026; not submitted or peer reviewed. The author explicitly discloses AI-assisted implementation, execution, analysis, and drafting. The claims concern a small synthetic benchmark, not a new general-purpose world-model architecture. Source: 15-page Adaptive World Models research manuscript. This page is an independently written account of source content—not a reproduction of its original display material.

Research question and motivation

When environment dynamics change suddenly, an AI planner can continue using an outdated model. This project asks whether neural-ensemble disagreement, observed transition surprise, and sequentially accumulated evidence recognize such shifts, and whether evidence-driven changes in planning horizon improve control performance or computational efficiency.

A central distinction in the study is that predictive uncertainty, change detection, model updating, and decision quality are different mechanisms. Detecting a change does not itself prove that subsequent actions will improve.

Environment, modeling assumptions, and baseline

The evaluation uses a synthetic finite-state navigation setting. It provides a known map, state coordinates, discrete directions, a goal-location heuristic, and transition observations; it is not an image-based or physical robot benchmark. The implementation models the direction of movement with a five-member neural transition ensemble. Each member has two hidden layers of width 32 and a four-category softmax output; the paper reports 1,412 parameters per network, 7,060 across the ensemble.

For each initial training seed, the study generates 6,000 random transitions, using 5,000 for training and 1,000 for validation. Ensemble members use bootstrap samples. Baselines include fixed lookahead horizons H2, H6, and H12, a disagreement-driven horizon, evidence-guided horizon, random horizon, a categorical counting model, and an evaluator-only true-model reference.

Three signals and change recognition

  • Predictive disagreement: the entropy of the ensemble-mean prediction minus the average entropy of the members, measuring model diversity before observing the next outcome.
  • Observed surprise: a moving average of negative log probability for actual observed directions, over up to 50 recent observations.
  • Sequential change evidence: accumulates calibrated indications that new observations no longer follow the earlier model, rather than responding to a single noisy reading.

The paper emphasizes that even an ensemble in agreement can be consistently wrong after a hidden change.

Planning, adaptation and computational work

The controller uses dynamic programming over 100 states, four intended actions, and four executed directions. It evaluates finite lookahead with discount factor γ = 0.98. The terminal value is a provided negative shortest-path distance to the goal. One action is executed, then the model replans; an evidence-based policy can shorten the planning horizon after detected change.

Model updating and horizon adaptation are studied separately, including a frozen-model ablation. Counted planning work measures Bellman transition contributions, not full wall-clock execution time.

Experiment design

Diagnostic Experiment 001 evaluated five seeds (100–104) under seven conditions, with 5,000 probes per condition: 175,000 diagnostic probes in total. These were development results and informed the detector design. The subsequent frozen evaluation used development seeds 0–2 and held-out seeds 200–207.

Each navigation run contains 1,200 interactions, with an environment-change boundary after interaction 400. The primary post-boundary reward averages interactions 401–1,200. Outcome categories include goal counts, hazards, timeouts, prediction total-variation (TV) distance, selected horizon, and counted computation.

In the diagnostic study, ensemble disagreement failed to flag five of five tested 0.05→0.35 changes within 250 observations, while uncalibrated rolling surprise flagged all five but also generated three false alarms in five low-noise stable controls. The manuscript reports that empirically calibrated evidence subsequently detected all eight evaluated global changes with no alarms in matched stable streams.

The principal result is not superior navigation reward; it is evidence of more efficient planning while separating accurate detection from decision quality.

Frozen model, limitations and reproducibility

The global-shift comparison reports evidence-guided online prediction TV near 0.197 versus 0.304 for the frozen variant, but improved model prediction did not establish improved reward. The manuscript notes a privileged true-model reference, one basic map and mirror, known coordinates and rewards, no partial observability or physical testing, different model biases across baselines, and limited seed/environment diversity.

The study documents a completed Windows reproduction on a CPU-compatible environment with 13 passing tests and a 1,200-step diagnostic run. It distinguishes the original benchmark, later local reproduction, and independent replication. Reported results remain unpublished and have not undergone external peer review.

Takeaways and further work

A controller can detect a dynamics change yet act no better than a fixed-horizon baseline. Future study directions identified in the manuscript include more varied maps, stronger and cost-matched comparators, better adaptation criteria, partial observability, and evaluation that tracks real computational cost as well as reward. The manuscript cites PETS and STEVE among model-based reinforcement-learning precedents but does not claim to reproduce those systems.
