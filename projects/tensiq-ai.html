TensiQ AI — Predictive Yarn-Tension Control

An industrial winding concept and benchtop architecture that combines yarn-tension sensing, machine-learning prediction, safety monitoring, and proactive motor-speed modulation.

Industrial problem and research question

Yarn-winding machines need controlled tension for stable package density. Excess tension can cause yarn breaks and downtime; low or inconsistent tension can form loose or uneven packages. Simple machines react when breakage is already occurring, while conventional threshold feedback can respond only after an excursion begins.

The key question is whether a low-cost microcontroller can identify early changes in tension behavior and proactively reduce winding speed before the thread breaks or package quality degrades.

Background benchmark and prior studies

The presentation discusses a May 2026 Egyptian cotton-facility study attributed to Ahmed, Amer, and Ismail, quoting a 35% reduction in winding defects and a 27% increase in winding speed. Those values describe a cited external study, not TensiQ performance.

It also references Azevedo et al. (2022) for offline fabric-break forecasting and Çörekcioğlu et al. (2026) for predictive approaches to sizing breakage. The proposed niche is coupling prediction to real-time winding motor actuation.

Benchtop mechanical design

  1. Supply spool and adjustable brake: source of controlled yarn and friction disturbances.
  2. Guide pulley and load cell: a 1-kg load-cell station measuring tension through an HX711 amplifier.
  3. Optical break sensor: independent immediate safety stop if yarn is lost.
  4. Take-up winding motor with encoder: provides rotational speed data and actuated PWM speed control.
  5. Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W: edge inference and telemetry controller.

The 3D CAD model describes a 1:2-scale miniature winding station. The presentation includes a visual model but the portfolio uses a textual description rather than reproducing the presentation graphics.

Three levels of intelligence

  • Detect: classify states as normal, low or excessive yarn tension.
  • Predict: inspect rising tension, changes in tension over time (dT/dt), and RPM-related patterns to anticipate instability.
  • Correct: apply bounded proactive deceleration and confirm tension recovery using continued sensor feedback.

The optical break detector remains a safety layer independent from any ML model decision.

Hardware constraints and calibration

The architecture uses the Pico 2 W's compact dual-core ARM platform to support a lightweight classifier and wireless telemetry. The 1-kg load-cell geometry is expected to amplify subtle friction-induced changes through its pulley wrap angle. HX711 data acquisition is chosen for relatively slow tension trends associated with friction and changing spool diameter; the presentation does not claim reliable capture of every high-frequency vibration.

Calibration tasks include dead-weight load reference checks, encoder RPM verification, bounded PWM output, and validation of the optical emergency-stop response.

Four-phase test roadmap

  1. Hardware assembly and mechanical/electrical calibration.
  2. Controlled dataset collection under friction changes and varying spool conditions.
  3. Deploy a compact classifier to the microcontroller, keeping independent break protection.
  4. Compare fixed-speed, conventional reactive and predictive modes using the same benchtop setup and unseen spool diameters.

The demonstration plan deliberately increases friction at the supply spool and looks for prediction, commanded slowdown, and eventual stabilization.

Engineering constraints and next evidence

Likely evaluation issues include load-cell noise, sampling-rate limits, the mapping between yarn force and pulley geometry, motor inertia, changing spool diameter, and how model inference interacts with hard motor safety limits. A complete technical dossier would need the final circuit diagram, trained model details, dataset description, motor-response logs, actual trial counts and results, and any failure cases.
