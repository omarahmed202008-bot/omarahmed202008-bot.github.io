Adaptive Climate Control in Smart Greenhouses

An ESP32-S3 dual-zone smart greenhouse with rule-based environmental control, independent irrigation, fault detection, logging and measured response tests under simulated arid-climate challenges.

Engineering challenge and objectives

Arid and hyper-arid agricultural environments have high thermal loads, water loss, and changes in vapor pressure deficit (VPD). This project aims to maintain greenhouse temperature, light, and root-zone moisture around crop-specific biological setpoints while reducing unnecessary use of water and energy.

The model targets two separate cultivation zones and emphasizes real-time response, independently controlled irrigation, cost accessibility, fault recognition, and automatic operation without frequent manual intervention.

Physical architecture and materials

The 0.25 m² prototype uses a recycled wood/acrylic structure, two opposing approximately 120-mm fans for crossflow cooling, two independently switched 12-V water pumps fed from a shared reservoir, supplemental RGB lighting, and a shared embedded control system.

  • ESP32-S3: controller and communication.
  • DHT22: greenhouse thermal and humidity monitoring.
  • Two capacitive soil-moisture sensors: zone-specific root moisture.
  • LDR: incident light indication.
  • INA219: power/current monitoring for actuator fault detection.
  • Relays, LM2596 DC buck converter, microSD and Wi-Fi telemetry: power interfacing and local/remote logs.

The cost table distinguishes materials already owned from a replacement purchase cost; its final estimate is about EGP 2,030 from scratch, with about EGP 1,280 in newly acquired items. The source assumes recycled or previously owned components in the assembled prototype.

Circuit integration and calibration

The team separated 12 V actuator power, converted 5 V peripheral power, and 3.3 V logic to limit sensor noise. Soil-sensor calibration used dry versus submerged reference readings and inverse linear interpolation: the poster reports ADC ~3100 at 0% relative water-content reference and ~1350 at 100% reference. DHT22 readings were offset/corrected relative to a reference thermometer; sensor placement was modified to reduce microcontroller heat bias. The LDR was calibrated to light-level reference conditions.

Electrical calculations use P = VI and E = Pt to connect measured loads with energy during correction cycles.

Control logic and logging

The ESP32-S3 executes a C++ rule-based control program, checking plant-specific thresholds for tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) and pepper (Capsicum annuum). The poster describes 5-minute data intervals and a target soil-water band near 20–40% VWC. When the measured threshold is breached, a fan, zone-specific pump or supplemental LED switches on and sensor feedback tracks recovery.

Local SD storage and Firebase are used for logging and fault alerts. A 6-hour data-logging test reported 72 discrete payloads, consistent with one payload every five minutes.

Test plan and controlled disturbances

  1. Calibrate soil, temperature, light and electrical sensors.
  2. Check continuous logging, Wi-Fi telemetry, and actuator response.
  3. Induce high-temperature conditions above ~32.5°C and measure forced-convection recovery.
  4. Induce drought below ~20% VWC in one zone and record independent irrigation response.
  5. Shade the structure to near 800–1000 lux and observe lighting response.
  6. Simulate mechanical failures, including a jammed cooling fan and an empty reservoir, and check current/power anomaly detection.
  7. Run or simulate a five-day outdoor-climate assessment with sampled morning, midday and evening measurements.

Results interpretation and limitations

Beyond recovering from disturbances, a notable result is detection of actuator faults using power signatures instead of assuming that a relay command guarantees physical operation. The poster describes both positive and negative trials, including a jammed fan that still required human intervention.

Limitations include LDR error under accumulated dust, dependency on Wi-Fi connectivity, reliance on fixed threshold bands that do not adapt to changing plants or VPD, and modest prototype scale. Five-day tests and climate simulation do not establish full seasonal agricultural performance or measured long-term crop yields.

Next design iterations and source context

The team proposes ML-guided prediction and adaptive threshold control as future work—not an implemented AI module. Other plans include shielding/redundancy for light sensors, LoRaWAN connectivity in remote fields, and a standalone solar/off-grid energy system. The poster cites FAO background, ESP32-S3 documentation, capacitive sensor calibration research, agricultural water monitoring, and vapor pressure deficit control literature.
